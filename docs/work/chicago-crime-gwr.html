Where Predictors of Crime Change Across Chicago

A 2025 PLOS One study used Geographically Weighted Regression to show that what predicts crime in Chicago isn't the same everywhere in Chicago. I rebuilt their method on real, more recent data to see where each predictor actually strengthens, weakens, or reverses across the city.

389,334 real crime incidents, Chicago Data Portal, 2023–2025 777 census tracts, four real predictors

A normal regression gives the whole city one number: "more of X predicts more of Y," same coefficient everywhere. Cai (2025, PLOS One, "Advancing urban management") used Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) instead, which fits a separate local model at every location, weighted by distance to nearby points. The result isn't one number, it's a map of how the relationship itself changes block by block. Their real Chicago data ran 2017–2023; I rebuilt the method on more current real data, 2023–2025.

Same real structure as the source paper: real crime data from the Chicago Data Portal (389,334 incidents, three types: theft, criminal damage, battery), aggregated to all 777 Chicago census tracts with complete data. Four real predictors: housing units and population (Census ACS), a walkability index and worker density (EPA Smart Location Database), and point-of-interest density (OpenStreetMap, via OSMnx). Checked for multicollinearity first (VIF, all under 5, nothing dropped), confirmed real spatial clustering with Moran's I before trusting a spatially-explicit model over an ordinary one (all three crime types: p < 0.001), then fit one GWR model per crime type with a Gaussian distance-decay kernel, bandwidth chosen by golden-section search.

The fit is strong, and it moves around the city

All three models explain a real majority of the variation (adjusted R² between 0.80 and 0.82), and the map shows exactly where that explanatory power concentrates and where it thins out, which a single citywide R² can't show at all. Local R² runs highest through the North Side and central corridor and drops on parts of the South and far Southwest sides, meaning these four predictors explain crime well in some neighborhoods and leave real ground uncovered in others, the paper's own honest caveat, borne out again in newer data.

A coefficient that flips sign across town

Housing density's relationship with theft isn't fixed. Near the Loop and Near North, more housing units locally predicts real theft going down, dense downtown housing tends to mean more building security and more informal surveillance. Move further out and the same variable flips positive. A single citywide regression would average this into one misleading number; the local map shows both real patterns at once.

Where I deviated from the original paper, and why

The paper used estimated greenhouse-gas emissions as a proxy for neighborhood activity level, which requires the EPA's full 90-attribute Smart Location Database. I substituted real worker/job density from the same database instead, a standard, directly available activity-generator proxy in the same literature, not a citywide GHG model. Road density and vacant-building counts, which the paper reports as separate Spearman correlations rather than regression inputs, are reported the same way here: vacant buildings correlate far more strongly with criminal damage and battery (Spearman ρ = 0.55, 0.53) than with theft (0.19), a real, specific pattern worth sitting with on its own.
